Operational Planning & Decision Support Document
1. Introduction
In container terminal operations, berth allocation is one of the most critical decisions.
Traditionally, berth selection is often based on availability and vessel size. However, this
approach does not consider:
● Where the cargo is located in the yard
● Travel distance for internal trucks (ITVs)
● Crane idle time risk
● Yard congestion
● Historical vessel productivity
● Conflict with other working vessels
The purpose of this document is to define a structured operational method to:
1. Identify the most suitable berth for an upcoming vessel
2. Predict expected port stay time
3. Detect operational conflict risks
4. Learn from previous vessel delays
5. Prepare the yard strategically before vessel arrival
The goal is simple:
Place the vessel in the best berth to minimize travel, reduce congestion, and
complete operations faster.

2. Berth Selection Based on Yard Cargo
Distribution
2.1 Operational Principle
When a vessel is scheduled to arrive, most of its loading containers are already inside the yard.
If the majority of these containers are located near a specific berth zone, placing the vessel at
that berth will:
● Reduce internal truck travel distance
● Reduce fuel consumption
● Reduce crane waiting time
● Reduce yard congestion
● Improve overall productivity
Therefore, berth selection should consider cargo location concentration.
2.2 Step 1 – Identify Loading Container Locations
Before confirming the berth, the system must:
1. Extract all containers nominated for loading.
2. Identify for each container:
○ Yard block
○ Yard row
○ Yard bay
○ Equipment class
○ Weight class
3. Map each yard block to its closest berth area.
This creates a clear picture of where the cargo is physically located.
2.3 Step 2 – Generate Yard Heat Map
A yard heat map must be created to visually represent container concentration.
The heat map should:
● Highlight blocks with high concentration (Red)
● Medium concentration (Orange)
--- PAGE 3 ---
● Low concentration (Green)
This visual tool allows planners to quickly see where the vessel’s cargo is clustered.
Example:
Terminal has:
● Berth 1
● Berth 2
● Berth 3
Vessel A has 1,500 loading containers.
After analysis:
● 78% located in blocks near Berth 1
● 40% located near Berth 2
● 25% located near Berth 3
Operational interpretation:
If the vessel is berthed at Berth 1:
● Minimal travel
● Lower congestion
● Higher crane efficiency
If berthed at Berth 3:
● Long travel distance
● Higher risk of ITV queue
● Increased crane idle time
2.4 Step 3 – Distance Impact Simulation
For each berth option, the system must calculate:
● Total Laden Travel Distance
(Yard Block → Selected Berth × Number of Containers)
● Estimated Unladen Travel
(Return trips, repositioning)
● Corridor congestion impact
This provides measurable data rather than opinion-based decisions.

3. Port Stay Time Prediction
3.1 Objective
To predict how long the vessel will remain at berth using historical performance data.
3.2 Operational Logic
Port stay depends on:
● Total moves (Load + Discharge)
● Number of cranes assigned
● Average crane productivity
● Crane intensity (how many cranes work simultaneously)
3.3 Calculation Method
Step 1: Estimate total moves
Total Moves = Planned Load + Planned Discharge
Step 2: Identify historical data for similar vessels:
● Average number of cranes assigned
● Average moves per hour per crane
--- PAGE 5 ---
Step 3: Calculate expected port stay:
Port Stay =
Total Moves ÷ (Number of Cranes × Average Productivity)
Example:
Total moves = 2,000
Cranes assigned = 4
Productivity = 30 moves/hour
Port Stay = 2000 ÷ (4 × 30) = 16.6 hours
3.4 Operational Benefit
This helps:
● Marine scheduling
● Manpower planning
● Crane allocation
● Berth window planning
● Customer communication
4. Berth Conflict & Crane Clash Analysis
4.1 Objective
To prevent operational conflicts when multiple vessels work simultaneously.
4.2 Operational Risks
Conflicts occur when:
● Two vessels load from same yard blocks
● Heavy ITV traffic in same corridors
● Crane rail overlap
--- PAGE 6 ---
● Equipment competition
These situations increase:
● Crane idle time
● Truck waiting time
● Vessel delay
4.3 Required Analysis
The system must check:
1. Percentage of shared yard blocks
2. Corridor overlap
3. Crane working zone overlap
4. Equipment demand conflicts
Output must classify risk:
● Low risk
● Medium risk
● High risk
And suggest mitigation:
● Change berth
● Adjust crane allocation
● Pre-consolidate yard cargo
5. Historical Delay Root Cause Analysis
5.1 Objective
To understand why previous vessels were delayed and avoid repeating mistakes.
5.2 Operational Checks
--- PAGE 7 ---
For previous delayed vessels, analyze:
● Crane idle time
● Container availability timing
● ITV arrival delays
● High M-cycle percentage
● Poor stacking strategy
● Long unladen travel
● Crane crossing conflicts
5.3 Interpretation
If delay reason = Container not ready
→ Improve yard readiness before arrival
If delay reason = High M-cycle
→ Improve stacking and consolidation
If delay reason = ITV congestion
→ Review corridor planning
Learning from history improves predictability.

Strategy
6.1 Objective
To prepare the yard before vessel arrival to reduce reshuffles and crane delay.
6.2 Historical Stowage Pattern Analysis

Based on prediction:
● Heavy containers positioned close to berth
● Light containers positioned for upper stack loading
● Group containers by discharge sequence
● Reduce reshuffle requirement
Expected result:

● Better dual cycle
